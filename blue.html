Ultramarine

The Blue Worth More Than Gold

Origin

Ultramarine comes from lapis lazuli, a semi-precious stone mined primarily from the Sar-e-Sang mines in Badakhshan province, Afghanistan. These mines have been worked since the 3rd millennium BCE.

Why Afghanistan?

The geological conditions required to form lazurite—the blue mineral within lapis—are rare. The Badakhshan deposits are among the few places on Earth where lapis lazuli forms in sufficient purity and quantity to be worth mining. The stone traveled ancient trade routes from Afghanistan throughout Europe and the Far East. The name ultramarine comes from the Latin ultra (beyond) and mare (sea), ultimately coming up with "from beyond the sea," reflecting its exotic origin.

History

Ultramarine was the most expensive pigment in Western Medieval and Renaissance painting. It was generally reserved for the robe of the Madonna or another prestigious figure, symbolizing both holiness and the patron's wealth. Artists used smalt (a cobalt glass pigment) as a cheaper substitute, but successful painters who could afford it preferred ultramarine. Nicholas Hilliard, the Elizabethan miniaturist, favored ultramarine for gems because of its pureness of color, hardness, and transparency. Rembrandt hardly used it—smalt was his choice. Natural ultramarine darkened with age, and its rarity meant that synthetic ultramarine was developed in 1826–1828. The synthetic version was chemically identical but cheap enough for any artist.

Hows Was It Made?

Extracting ultramarine from lapis lazuli was a time-consuming, multi-step process:

  1. Powdered lapis was mixed with wax, resin, and oils.
  2. The mixture was kneaded in a dilute lye solution for days or weeks.
  3. Blue particles dispersed into the alkaline water, while extraneous minerals (calcite, pyrite, silicates) remained in the putty.
  4. The purest, deepest blue was extracted in the first batch and sold for very high prices.
  5. Subsequent batches produced slightly less pure pigments; the final batch was a transparent blue-gray called ultramarine ash.

The chemical composition is a complex sodium aluminum silicate sulfide: 3Na₂O·3Al₂O₃·6SiO₂·2Na₂S. The blue color comes from polysulfide ions trapped in the mineral's crystal structure.

What Makes It Dangerous?

Ultramarine has no significant hazards and is:

  • Noncombustible
  • Insoluble in water
  • No known toxicity

The pigment discolors when exposed to weak acids or sulfur fumes. It becomes dull with outdoor weathering. For artists, ultramarine was safe—the only "danger" was financial ruin from its cost.

Artists Who Used Ultramarine

  • Renaissance painters of the Virgin Mary
    Ultramarine was the standard for Mary's robe. The pigment's cost meant its presence in a painting signaled both the artist's skill and the patron's wealth.
  • Nicholas Hilliard (ca. 1547-1619)
    The Elizabethan miniaturist preferred ultramarine for gems, valuing its pureness of color, hardness, and transparency.
  • Girolamo Romanino (ca. 1484-1566)
    Used smalt as a cheaper substitute for ultramarine in The Nativity (ca. 1524), layering it over azurite to mimic the more expensive blue.

Famous Works Featuring Ultramarine

  • The Virgin and Child
    Numerous Renaissance examples
  • Medieval illuminated manuscripts
    Reserved for sacred figures
  • Renaissance altarpieces
    Mary's robe as a status symbol
  • Works by Titian and Veronese
    Used for prestigious blue passages
