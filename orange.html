Cadmium Orange

The Alchemist's Accident

Origin

Cadmium was discovered in Germany in 1817, not through deliberate search but through pharmaceutical quality control. The story is tangled. In early 19th-century Prussia, government physicians inspected medicines for purity. Johann Roloff became suspicious of zinc oxide from Karl Hermann's factory, suspecting arsenic contamination. Meanwhile, Friedrich Stromeyer, Inspector-General of apothecaries in neighboring Hanover, was investigating zinc carbonate samples that left a yellow oxide upon heating. Both men, corresponding and writing about their findings, converged on the same conclusion: an unknown metal was present. Stromeyer proposed the name cadmium, from "cadmia," the Latin term for calamine (zinc-based minerals).

History

Cadmium's path to the artist's palette was swift. By the 1840s, cadmium compounds were being produced as pigments—brilliant yellows, oranges, and reds derived from cadmium sulfide (CdS) and cadmium sulfoselenides. The timing was perfect. Artists lacked stable, lightfast oranges and reds. Earlier pigments faded, darkened, or reacted with other colors. Cadmium pigments offered exceptional hiding power, light-fastness, and stability—qualities that made them instant favorites. Stromeyer himself developed the first cadmium orange by tweaking how he combined cadmium with sulfur, moving from the yellow sulfide to a more orange-toned compound.

How Was It Made?

Cadmium pigments are synthesized by heating cadmium salts in hydrogen sulfide gas until a powdery precipitate forms.
The color depends on chemical composition:

  1. Cadmium sulfide (CdS) alone produces yellow
  2. Cadmium sulfoselenides (replacing some sulfur with selenium) shift the color toward orange and red.
  3. The physical reason: these compounds are semiconductors with band gaps in the visible spectrum. CdS has a bandgap of 2.42 eV, absorbing blue and violet light and reflecting yellow. Adding selenium narrows the bandgap, shifting the reflected color toward orange and red.

What Makes It Dangerous?

Cadmium is highly toxic. The element has no known biological function in higher organisms. It accumulates in the body, particularly in the kidneys and liver, causing:

  • Kidney damage
  • Bone disease (itai-itai disease)
  • Lung cancer
  • Reproductive harm

Modern regulations have become increasingly stringent. Cadmium salts are being replaced by azo compounds in commercial paints, and artists are advised to use cadmium-free alternatives wherever possible. The pigment is dangerous through inhalation of dust and skin contact. Historical artists, unaware of these risks, handled cadmium pigments without protection.

Artists Who Used Cadmium Orange

  • Claude Monet
    He used cadmium orange for the vivid-glowing orange sun in Impression, Sunrise (1872)—the painting that gave the Impressionist movement its name. The pigment's intensity and stability made it ideal for capturing the transient effects of light that defined Impressionism.
  • Leon Bakst
    A russian costume designer, used cadmium orange in sketches for the Ballet Boutique Fantasque (1917), illustrating the exact color he wanted for a bonnet.
  • Donal Judd
    An american minimalist who used cadmium orange on his sculptures, embracing its industrial brightness.

Famous Works Featuring Cadmium Orange
